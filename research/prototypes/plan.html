What's strong and buried

The recon turned up a lot that deserves to be on the front page and currently isn't. He spent 2006–2013 at NOAA's Office of Hydrologic Development in Silver Spring working on the US National Weather Service's ensemble streamflow forecasting service, then the World Bank and Johns Hopkins, before IIT Hyderabad in Feb 2017. He was Head of the Department of Climate Change 2019–2022 and chairs the Rural Development Centre. Twenty-nine refereed papers; Google Scholar shows 1,859 citations and an h-index of 15.

SnapFlood — a citizen flood-reporting app with 100+ installs, funded by DST-SPLICE, the National Network Programme on Urban Climate and the Government of India's AI Centre of Excellence for Sustainable Cities, covered by NewsMeter, The Hans India and Deccan Chronicle in 2023 — sits three clicks deep. None of those funders or articles is named anywhere on the site. There is even a mascot, VarshaMitra, that appears only in a YouTube description.

The single highest-value thing missing

Eighteen graduates are listed with thesis titles and viva dates and no placement information at all. Where alumni went is the strongest evidence a research group can publish, and it's the first thing a prospective PhD student looks for. It exists nowhere public — only he and they know it. Collecting it is a WhatsApp round, and I want to start it on day one rather than at the end.

Section twoThe direction

You said it must not look AI-made. That's not a styling problem — it's a sourcing problem. Generic sites look generic because their form comes from a template library instead of from their subject. So the form here comes from the documents his own field publishes.

The reference points are the documents his own field publishes — a National Weather Service river page, a Central Water Commission flood bulletin, a USGS topographic sheet that sets river names in italic and puts its provenance in a dense block at the foot. What we take from them is structure, not decoration: numbers in fixed columns, separations drawn as real hairlines, no persuasion anywhere.

The masthead is type, not a graphic. The first draft opened with a hydrograph carrying the 1908 Musi flood as a datum line, and you were right that it wouldn't land — it asked him for data he may not hold and leaned on a flood that killed fifteen thousand people. It's gone. The page now opens with his name, a hard rule, one paragraph, and a photograph. Quieter, and it depends on nothing we have to ask him for.

The prototype is built — two pages, with his real photographs in it. Open it before reading further; it argues better than this paragraph does.

The decisions that make it non-generic

  • The photographs are the anchor, and they're his. The portrait has the irrigation map of Telangana on the wall behind him — a hydrologist in front of a map of his own state's water, which is worth more than any studio headshot. The group shots show people actually working: laptops, a camera on the table, the SnapFlood site on a monitor. Nothing is posed and nothing is stock.
  • The VarshaMitra illustration carries the SnapFlood page. Hand-drawn, signed by the artist, and it shows exactly what the app is for: a girl in a yellow VarshaMitra kurta photographing a flooded street while a child floats paper boats. It is the single most un-templatable asset in the project.
  • The accent has a citation. #166157 is a stop on the IPCC AR6 precipitation colormap. #8C5A24 is the dry pole of the same divergent ramp, and on this site it means absence — an alumnus whose placement we don't have, a paper with no open copy, a project with no public data. His whole argument is that the gap is in the observations; here the gaps have a colour, and it's the one his discipline uses for drier.
  • Named water bodies render as hydrography. Musi, Godavari, Narmada, Krishna, Osman Sagar — italic, in the accent, automatically, everywhere in prose, from a term list. No template can do this, because no template knows which nouns are rivers.
  • Student co-authors are marked automatically. * PhD, † M.Tech, derived by matching author strings against the group roster and checking the paper's year against when they were in the group. He never types anything about students; adding a student retroactively marks every paper they co-authored.
  • The ground is tinted and the figures are the white. Every academic site is a white page with grey cards. This is the reverse. It costs one colour token and it's legible from across a room.
  • His career is drawn, not listed. Washington and Baltimore overlap; in a line of type that overlap reads as a typing error, and as four bars on a shared axis it's the interesting part.
  • Products are a first-class section, built for a list that grows. Seven already exist — SnapFlood, UFIS, GHMC-RADS, VarshaAnalytics, Hydro-EMOS, Extreme Climate Analysis, the poverty tool. Each is one row, so the eighth costs nothing. Very few academic sites treat shipped software as a peer of publications, and his does because it should.
  • Zero JavaScript, zero third-party requests, zero cookies. Filters are real URLs, sidenotes are <details>, the nav wraps instead of collapsing into a drawer. The whole page is about 115 KB warm. The current Google Site ships 135 KB of empty HTML shell before any content.

Three departures from the research brief

The agents proposed numbering the nav items 01 RESEARCH · 02 PUBLICATIONS, tracked-out small-caps labels above each section, and monospace metadata everywhere. Those are exactly the reflexes that read as templated in 2026 — numbering implies a sequence the nav doesn't have. I dropped all three: journal names sit in italic the way journals actually set them, and mono is reserved for things that are genuinely measured — the issue stamp, the status line, axis labels, the footer.

Calls made without asking

  • RAFT expands to "Rainfall-runoff Analysis and Forecasting Tools." His site says "Analysis modelling and Forecasting Tools", which isn't English and doesn't spell RAFT. The shorter form is already public on the Play Store listing. We should also fix that listing — it currently credits the developer as "Suresh Regonda."
  • No citation counts on the site. Scholar, OpenAlex, Crossref and Scopus report four different numbers for the same paper. Anything we hard-code is wrong within a month. A quiet link to his Scholar profile instead.
  • No dark mode. The site is a paper object, and the colophon says so in one line. The forecast-category colours a dark mode would have used are spent on flood-event mode instead, where they carry information.
  • The blog doesn't exist until he writes. At zero posts the nav item, the index page and the RSS feed are simply not built. A blog whose newest post is eighteen months old is worse than no blog.
  • No phone number, anywhere. Email only, plus the office room. His mobile is on the CV and on the last slide of the SnapFlood deck; it will not be on the website.
  • No CV page and no CV file. /about/ carries education, appointments and funded projects in web form. Nothing to leak, nothing to go stale.
  • The "Integrated System View" slide is not usable. It's a generic stock architecture diagram — external data → core application logic → API responses — that says nothing about UFIS. The real diagram is slide 4, which is 31 separate PowerPoint shapes, so I redrew it from its actual content in the site's own type and palette. It's in the prototype.

Flood-event mode

Journalist and agency traffic spikes during a flood, and a site that looks identical on a dry Tuesday and during a Musi flood is wasting its best moment. Three cells in the spreadsheet — a state, one sentence, and an end time — reorder the home page: the status strip goes to the top in the forecast-category colour, SnapFlood and the press contact rise above everything, the recruiting line drops down, and the news rail shows only the last fortnight.

Two rules keep it safe. The site never issues a forecast of its own — it prints a dated sentence he wrote and links to IMD, TSDPS, GHMC and CWC, with a permanent line saying it isn't an official warning. And it cannot get stuck on: the end time is mandatory, capped at four days, and the build reverts to normal whether or not he remembers.

The split with raftwaters.com

Now that the group has its own domain, the division is clean and worth stating once so it doesn't blur later:

  • This site is about him — his research, his publications, his teaching, his students, the products he leads. First person throughout.
  • raftwaters.com is about the group — the full roster, group news, the press kit, outreach, campaigns. Third person.
  • /group/ here is a summary and a door. What RAFT is, one working photograph, the roster, alumni, and "for everything else, raftwaters.com". The outbound URL is a single spreadsheet cell, so if the domain ever changes it's a one-cell edit.

Press follows the same split. His site keeps a short In the press list — five dated, linked items, no downloads and no canned quotes — because a journalist who searched his name during a flood needs to see he's quotable and reachable without a second hop. The full kit, with portraits and boilerplate, belongs on the group site. Institutional roles like the Rural Development Centre chairmanship sit under Appointments on /about/, not in news.

One thing to fix before we link anything: raftwaters.com currently serves GoDaddy's auto-generated placeholder — stock photo, Playfair Display, "Ride the Rapids with Us." Until the real site replaces it, a link from his page would do active harm.

The applicant form

A form on /join/ posting through Apps Script into an Applicants tab, which is the same Google account and the same spreadsheet as everything else — no new service, no new login.

The reason it's worth building rather than just printing his email: it makes his own filter a required field. His current Opportunities page asks people to read one of his papers and say what question it raised. In an email that request is optional and mostly ignored. As a required textarea it is the price of the send button, and he can sort the sheet by that column.

Three things keep it honest: it works with JavaScript disabled (a plain form POST; the kilobyte of script only avoids bouncing the visitor to a Google confirmation page); a hidden honeypot field plus a submission-time check stops most bots without a CAPTCHA; and the mailto stays visible underneath, so nobody is forced through the form to reach him.

The parts that decide whether this survives

  • Settings is a key/value tab, not a wide row. A single row under a header is the layout most vulnerable to what people actually do — insert a row, sort, add a column — any of which shifts every value one cell across and silently publishes the department name into the email field. Key/value is immune to all three and reads on a phone.
  • One person, one row. Current students and alumni are the same tab with a status column, rendered three ways. Changing current to alumni moves someone from the roster to the alumni table. The two lists can't disagree — which is precisely the bug his site has today.
  • Errors appear in the sheet, before any build runs. Cross-tab formulas put a red ❌ next to a misspelled student name or a duplicate ID the instant it's typed. For a non-technical editor, catching it in the spreadsheet beats catching it in a build log every time.
  • Never delete a row — tick exclude. If he deletes one anyway, the old page stays live with a noindex tag so citation links keep working, and the email explains what to do instead.
  • Photographs are picked, never pasted. A menu item picks the file from his Images folder, so there is no Drive link to get wrong. Alt text and a photographer credit are required — the site refuses to publish a photograph without them, on purpose.
  • Consent is a column. A student's photograph and email publish only if the box is ticked. Removal on request is: clear two cells, press publish.

The one part I'm still wary of

Google Docs → clean web HTML is the fragile link in an all-Google setup. Docs export carries no alt text on images, mangles pasted formatting, and turns smart quotes into noise. The design answer is to constrain and reject rather than parse and hope: a defined vocabulary of headings, bold, italic, links, lists, quotes and images-with-captions. Anything outside it — a table, a footnote, a smart chip — and the build refuses that one document by name, in plain English, and publishes everything else normally. He loses one page, never the site.

Section fiveHow publishing works

He ticks a box. About three minutes later the site is live. He can do it from the Sheets app on his phone, which is the point — the recruiting notice and the flood line are exactly what you want to change from an airport.

01  Trigger

Tick publish_now in Settings — it unticks itself. Or a menu item on a laptop. Or nothing: it republishes itself at 01:30 and 13:30 daily, hourly during a flood event.

02  Fetch & check

One API call reads all 18 tabs. Every field is validated against a schema. A missing tab or column stops the build; a single bad row is skipped with a warning and everything else publishes.

03  Commit

The content is written back into the code repository as plain Markdown files, and the images are downloaded and optimised. This is the decision that makes the site outlive Google, and us.

04  Deploy

Static files to Cloudflare's edge — 22 Indian locations, Hyderabad among them. Then an email to him in plain English saying what published, or what didn't and why.

Because every publish commits the content back as Markdown, the repository is always a complete, working copy of the site that needs no Google account at all. Flip one environment variable and it rebuilds from those files with no network access. That claim gets tested before launch — someone else clones it on a clean machine with no credentials and rebuilds the site — because until it's been done it's only a claim.
