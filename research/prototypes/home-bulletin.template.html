For seven years I forecast rivers across the continental United States. Since February 2017 I have done it for one city, Hyderabad, and for the Godavari and the Narmada.

At NOAA's Office of Hydrologic Development in Silver Spring I worked on the US National Weather Service's Hydrologic Ensemble Forecast Service — the post-processing that turns one streamflow number into a distribution a reservoir operator can act on. I came to IIT Hyderabad to do the same work for Indian rivers, and I have ended up doing most of it for one city.

Hyderabad floods for reasons that are only partly about the drainage. Rain here arrives in short intense bursts over a few square kilometres, and our gauge network was built to measure a different kind of rain. Afterwards we have almost no record of where the water actually stood. My group works at both ends of that gap — the meteorology and hydrology that produce the flood, and the data collection that would let anyone check whether we got it right.

How much water will Indian cities and rivers see, and how soon can we tell anyone?

RAFT — Rainfall-runoff Analysis and Forecasting Tools — collects, models and forecasts rainfall and runoff for Indian cities and river basins, and turns it into information people can act on. Nine PhD students and two M.Tech students work in the group; eighteen have graduated since 2019.

SnapFlood

Snap, upload, contribute. A photograph of a flooded street, with a location and a time, is a measurement — and it is the measurement India's flood record is missing.

Google Play · App Store · snapflood-web · Free · Telugu and English

It started as a hashtag

In 2023 the group ran VarshaMitra — friend of the rain — asking people in Hyderabad to tweet flooding as it happened. The ask was specific, because a photograph without a place and a depth is not data. They called it a Sixer Tweet: one tweet, six tags.

Tag 1
City #Hyderabadrains #Hyderabadfloods
Tag 2
Landmark — apartment or building name
Tag 3
Locality — colony or street name #Koti
Tag 4
Water depth #kneewater #5cm
Tag 5
Date #20072023
Tag 6
Time #0800

A tweet reading "railway bridge at Lingampally with full of water" tells you almost nothing. The same event, tagged six ways, gives a location, a geolocation, a depth of five to six centimetres, and a timestamp of 08:00 on 20 July 2023. That is a row in a database.

Twitter was never going to hold it. So the campaign became an app — the same six questions, asked by a form instead of a hashtag, with the location taken from the phone. That is SnapFlood.

How it fits together

SnapFlood is one input to a larger system. The Urban Flood Information System joins what citizens see to what satellites, gauges and models produce, and turns it into something a city can act on.

Not a replacement for a city's existing cameras, sensors and models — an additional layer of ground-level evidence.

  1. Someone photographs a flooded street and uploads it with a location and a time.
  2. That observation joins satellite rainfall, weather-station records and the city's own elevation, drainage and land-use data.
  3. Hydrological, hydraulic and machine-learning models turn the combination into an estimate of where water is and where it is going.
  4. The estimate becomes a dashboard, an inundation map, a risk map, or an alert.
  5. GHMC and the state agencies use it to locate flooding, prioritise response and plan drainage — and what people report next is shaped by what they were told.
